The Edge — Approaching Hard Vacuum
Above 80,000 ft · pressure under ~28 hPa · this is where intuition fails

The overheating paradox

Everyone insulates against cold. Almost nobody expects their electronics to overheat at 100,000 ft — but with almost no air, there is almost no convective cooling. A GoPro dissipating several watts inside a sealed foam box is now a camera in a thermos. Heat leaves only by radiation and conduction into the box. Cameras that ran cool at 40k can throttle, glitch, or shut down near apogee.

  • Don't hermetically seal warm electronics. A small vent path or thermal mass (heatsink bonded to the camera body) matters more up here than another layer of foam.
  • Sealed bags and containers inflate and pop — a chip bag at launch is a balloon at 90k. Any closed volume needs a vent hole.
  • Liquids boil at body temperature up here. Anything wet — gel packs, certain adhesives — behaves differently near vacuum.

Chemical hand warmers stop working

The classic beginner move: toss hand warmers in the payload box. They're iron-oxidation packs that need oxygen — and above 80k there's very little. They fade exactly when you think you need them. If you want heat, budget battery watts for a resistor, or rely on insulation plus the electronics' own dissipation.

The Deep Freeze — Tropopause & Lower Stratosphere
The coldest part of the flight is NOT the top

Cold soak happens in the middle

Counterintuitive but critical: air temperature bottoms out around −55 to −65 °C near the tropopause (~36k–50k ft), then actually warms slightly as you climb into the stratosphere. Your payload spends 30–60 minutes soaking in the coldest air of the mission on the way up — and again, briefly, on the way down at high descent speed. Design for the middle of the flight, not the top.

Batteries — the number one flight killer

  • Alkalines are dead weight. Their chemistry collapses in deep cold. The hobby standard is lithium primary AAs (Energizer Ultimate Lithium) — rated to −40 °C, light, and flat discharge curve.
  • LiPo packs sag hard in cold. Voltage droop under transmit load is how trackers brown-out mid-flight: the radio keys up, voltage dips, the processor resets. Insulate the pack, keep it against the warmest component, and bench-test TX under load in your freezer before you fly.
  • Capacity math: whatever runtime you measured on the bench at 22 °C, plan on meaningfully less. Fly margin — if the mission is 3 hours, power for 8. The chase sometimes takes longer than the flight.

Cameras and lenses

  • Lens fog and frost: moist cabin air from your launch site condenses on cold glass. Assemble and seal the payload in dry conditions, use anti-fog inserts or desiccant, and give the lens a clear view outside the box rather than shooting through a window that will frost.
  • Record time beats resolution. A dead battery at 60k on the way up means no burst footage. Big card, conservative settings, external battery pack if the camera supports it.

Radio through the cold

  • Cheap crystal oscillators drift with temperature — your downlink can walk off frequency during cold soak. TCXO-equipped trackers hold the line.
  • Coax and plastic mounts get brittle and stiff; strain-relieve everything, because the burst event is violent and the whole train snaps like a whip.

APRS etiquette — you are the loudest station in four states

From altitude, your little tracker is heard directly by every digipeater and iGate for hundreds of miles. That changes the rules, and getting them wrong makes you famous for the wrong reasons:

  • Set your path to zero hops. No WIDE1-1, no WIDE2-1 — nothing. A ground station needs digipeaters to be heard; you don't. If you fly a repeated path, hundreds of digipeaters that all hear you directly will each rebroadcast every beacon, flooding the shared 144.390 channel across multiple states with thousands of duplicate packets. Your packet already reaches the iGates on the first transmission. It's rude.
  • Don't wait for a clear channel. Carrier-sense ("listen before transmit") is good manners on the ground — but at 60k your receiver hears every APRS transmission in the entire footprint at once. The channel is never clear from up there, and a polite tracker will sit silent all flight waiting for its turn. Transmit on a fixed schedule — every 60 seconds or so — and let the timing do the sharing.
  • You don't need power to match your reach: a watt or two from altitude is plenty. Line-of-sight does the work; the battery savings go to flight margin.

The Working Atmosphere — Launch to 30k
Where weather, airspace, and rigging mistakes live

GPS above 60,000 ft — check before you buy

Export rules (the old COCOM limits) mean many consumer GPS modules stop reporting above ~60,000 ft. Some freeze, some go silent until descent. The hobby standard is a u-blox module set to airborne/high-altitude dynamic model. This is the single most common "why did my tracker go quiet at 60k" question in the hobby — solve it on the bench, not in the field. (It bites in the band above, but you have to fix it down here.)

Airspace is a system, not a suggestion

  • Know FAR Part 101 cold — payload weight limits, weight/size-per-package rules, and when you're exempt vs. when you're filing.
  • Call the ATC facility, file the NOTAM, and launch when you said you would. Your flight passes through altitudes where airliners live — twice.
  • Pick launch sites and forecast days so the predicted track avoids Class B, airports, cities, and big water. Run the prediction for several days around your window; if the landing zone is ugly, scrub. The balloon doesn't care about your schedule.

Rigging and the train

  • Order matters: balloon → line → parachute (pre-deployed, inline) → line → payload. After burst, balloon remnants fall with the train and love to foul the chute — leave generous spacing.
  • Gloves and ground sheet at fill: skin oils weaken latex, and a Texas stubble field will find the one thorn your balloon can't survive.
  • Every knot taped or sewn. The burst shock at −60 °C is the test your rigging either passes or doesn't.

Down & Dirty — Descent and Recovery
The mission isn't over at burst — half the failures happen now

The descent is fast, then slow

In thin air the chute barely bites: descent from 100k starts at very high speed and only settles to your sea-level rate as the air thickens. That's normal — don't panic when the tracker shows a screaming fall at 90k. But it means the burst shock and early descent are the structural stress test.

Condensation, round two

A payload cold-soaked to −40 °C descending into warm, humid Texas air fogs and drips instantly — inside and out. Expect wet electronics at recovery; design so a little condensation isn't fatal, and get the SD cards out and dry early.

Recovery realities

  • Redundant tracking: APRS for the flight, plus a cheap independent tracker (SPOT, LoRa, or a phone in a pinch) for the last hundred feet — APRS coverage near the ground in ranch country is spotty exactly when you need it most.
  • Put a label on the payload: name, phone number, "harmless science experiment," and a small reward line. Payloads land in strangers' fields; make the stranger your ally.
  • Trees eat payloads. A telescoping pole in the chase car has saved more missions than any piece of electronics.
  • Landowner permission before you hop a fence — in Texas especially. A friendly knock recovers payloads; a trespass call ends hobbies.

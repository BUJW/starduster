Your first flight footage will be a spinning, swinging mess.
Here's the physics — and the fixes.

Nothing on a balloon train is rigid, powered, or steered. Everything that keeps your horizon level is passive physics you rig on the ground. Six ideas below — each one is a lesson most people learn by watching two hours of rotating footage.

01 · The enemy

Why everything spins in the first place

A rising balloon isn't a clean teardrop — it sheds vortices as air slips around it, and each shed gives it a tiny rotational kick. Those kicks add up, the balloon rotates, and the line below it winds up like a rubber band, storing torque. Eventually that torque unwinds straight into your camera. There is no "still" on a balloon train; there is only managed rotation.

The fix — you can't stop the spin at the source. You isolate it (swivels), resist it (inertia), and slow it (train length). That's the next three panels.
02 · Resist it

Long arms: the figure-skater trick, reversed

I = m·r² — inertia grows with the square of distance

A skater pulls their arms in to spin faster. You do the opposite: push mass out. Cameras on the ends of a boom put mass far from the spin axis, and because rotational inertia scales with distance squared, doubling the arm length quadruples the resistance to spin. The same wound-up line torque that whips a compact box now barely turns the boom.

Bonus: two cameras at opposite ends balance each other, so the boom hangs level instead of tilting toward the heavy side.

The fix — a rigid boom with cameras (or ballast) at the tips. Symmetric, stiff, and as long as your rigging and Part 101 weight budget allow.
03 · Isolate it

Swivels are torque firewalls

A ball-bearing fishing swivel costs a dollar and weighs nothing, and it does one job perfectly: it refuses to transmit torque. The line above it can wind up all it wants — the swivel spins freely and the twist never crosses the joint. The balloon keeps its rotation; your camera doesn't inherit it.

Use more than one. Standard placement: below the balloon (kills the biggest torque source), and above the payload (catches anything the train itself generates). On descent the parachute becomes the new spin source, so the swivel above the payload earns its keep twice.

The fix — quality ball-bearing swivels (cheap barrel swivels bind under load and quietly stop swiveling), one under the balloon, one over the payload.
04 · Slow it

Long trains swing slower — by law

T = 2π·√(L/g)

Your payload is a pendulum and the balloon is the pivot. Every wind-shear layer you climb through kicks that pendulum. The period depends on only the length — not the weight — so a 5 m train swings a full cycle every ~4.5 seconds (nauseating on video), while a 15 m train takes ~7.8 seconds and drifts through smaller angles for the same kick.

Long trains also separate the dynamics: balloon wobble, chute breathing, and payload sway become different slow rhythms instead of one coupled mess, and each line segment flexes a little, bleeding energy out of the system.

The fix — 10–20 m of train. It costs a few grams of line and makes launch clumsier, and it's still the cheapest image stabilization ever invented.
05 · The trap

Fins work on the ground and quit at altitude

F_aero ∝ ρ·v² — and ρ is ~2% at 90k ft

The intuitive fix for spin is aerodynamic: add fins, vanes, or streamers so the payload "weathervanes" straight. It works beautifully in your backyard test — and then fades to nothing as the air does. At 90,000 ft your carefully tuned fin is pushing on 2% of the air it was designed for, while the line-torque physics keeps working at full strength.

This is why the passive-mechanical fixes — inertia, swivels, train length — are the ones veterans trust: they don't need air to work.

The fix — treat aero stabilizers as a bonus for the lower atmosphere, never the primary plan. If it only works with air, it doesn't work at apogee.
06 · The last 10%

Jello: the vibration you can't see on the ground

Swinging and spinning are slow; jello is fast. Wind strums the train lines like guitar strings, and a boom has resonant frequencies of its own. Your camera's rolling shutter reads the sensor line-by-line, so high-frequency vibration turns straight horizons into wobbling soup — even when the payload looks rock-steady.

The counterintuitive part: soft foam alone can make it worse by letting the camera buzz on its own little suspension. What kills jello is coupling the camera rigidly to real mass (the whole payload box), so the vibration has to shake everything to shake the sensor — and keeping line attachment points away from the middle of unsupported spans.

The fix — bolt the camera hard to the heaviest structure you have, damp the lines (tape tag-ends, avoid drum-tight rigging), and test by shaking the finished payload, not the bare camera.

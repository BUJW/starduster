is the tallest tower in North America.

People ask why a balloon hobby needs a ham license. The honest answer: altitude is the most valuable thing in radio, and a balloon buys more of it for $50 than any antenna tower ever built. Here's the why behind the callsigns.

01Why radio at all?

Your phone quits early — cell antennas point down at people, not up at balloons, and coverage above a few thousand feet is luck at best. Satellite trackers (SPOT and friends) work, and they make a fine backup, but they hand you a position every few minutes with no story in between.

APRS gives you telemetry. Position, altitude, speed, and whatever sensors you wire in, every minute, received by strangers' stations across the state and piped to a live map anyone can watch. The chase crew, your family at home, and a retired ham in Oklahoma you've never met are all watching the same dot. The radio isn't just tracking — it's the part of the mission other people can participate in. That's the ham radio ethos, and it's why the license is the price of admission.

02APRS in one paragraph

APRS is a shared national digital channel — 144.390 MHz in the US — where stations beacon short data packets. Ground stations called digipeaters rebroadcast weak packets so they travel farther, and iGates bridge everything they hear onto the internet, which is how your balloon shows up on aprs.fi in real time. A car in a valley needs digipeater hops to be heard. Your balloon has the opposite problem — after the first 1,000 feet it's line-of-sight to dozens of iGates simultaneously, and by 30,000 feet, hundreds. Altitude solves propagation completely; the etiquette challenge it creates is covered on the lessons page.

04The crossband repeater — a borrowed mountaintop

A repeater listens on one frequency and retransmits on another, so two stations that can't hear each other can both talk through it. Clubs put them on mountains and towers because a repeater is only as good as its view. A balloon at 90,000 feet has a better view than every mountaintop repeater on the continent — for a couple of hours.

The non-obvious part is the crossband. A same-band repeater (listen on 2 m, talk on 2 m) has to hear a whisper while shouting on a nearby frequency, which takes bulky cavity duplexers — far too heavy to fly. Listening on 70 cm and retransmitting on 2 m (or the reverse) separates receive and transmit by hundreds of MHz, so a tiny handheld radio can do the whole job with no duplexer at all. That's why crossband is the balloon repeater architecture: it's the only one that fits in a lunchbox.

The footprint is mutual and it moves. Two stations can only talk if both are inside the balloon's horizon circle at that moment — so the usable repeater coverage grows through the whole ascent, peaks at burst, then collapses on descent faster than people expect. Working a station 600 miles away through a lunchbox at the edge of space, knowing the window is closing, is the whole thrill.

Coming to Mission Control — the live flight map will get a toggleable footprint ring drawn from current APRS altitude, so during a flight you can watch the theoretical coverage grow and shrink in real time, and chase crews know when they're inside the repeater window.

05Why it answers "why"

Cameras justify the flight to non-hams. Radio justifies it to physics. Every part of this page — the footprint math, the crossband architecture, the etiquette of being the loudest station in four states — is applied RF engineering you can't learn as viscerally any other way. The balloon is a two-hour license upgrade.
